Are you hitting the target with your wine comms?

08 September 2026 By Roger Morris

It may sound obvious but wine producers should be tailoring specific messages for different audiences. And it’s easier than you think to miss the mark, writes Roger Morris.

No matter where they are based, winegrowers are increasingly being asked to communicate key marketing messages with various audiences across many venues – in their tasting rooms, during group visits to their wineries, at walk-around tastings, via Zoom in virtual sessions, as guests during podcasts, and in pitches and press releases made by their PR agencies and via social media.

But are the right messages being given to the right audiences – to consumers wherever they are encountered, to trade, including importers, distributors, shop owners and restaurateurs, as well as to media and influencers? And is the right person communicating these messages to each audience?

Changing times

Gone are the days when a single message could satisfy everyone.

“The starting point is that the core message does not change,” says Josè Rallo, co-owner of Sicily’s internationally distributed Donnafugata estate. “Donnafugata tells the story of Sicily through wine, its territories, its biodiversity, its culture and a contemporary vision of sustainability. What changes, depending on the audience, is the language, the level of depth and the type of information that becomes most relevant.”

Veteran American PR executive and owner of Ten27 Communications, Amy Mironov, who has represented dozens of international wine and spirits producers, agrees with Rallo.

“Consumers, retailers, beverage directors, distributors and journalists all have different priorities and motivations,” she says. “The most successful producers understand those differences and adapt their messaging while remaining true to the core story of the wine and the brand.”

Three magic letters – DTC

There was a time when wine drinkers got information about wines and wine producers almost totally via wine publications and through hand-selling by their local wine merchants. Liberalisation of wine shipping laws, the growth of the internet, social media influencers and the explosion of wine tourism changed all that.

While visits to tasting rooms have dipped slightly in recent years, they remain the one-place where seller most often meets drinker face to face. And consumer impressions here and with the winery’s back story may be as important as the taste and price of the wines being poured.

Jon Ruel, president of Early Mountain Vineyards in Virginia, says that experience “begins with framing our wines, both the varieties and the styles, in the context of wines they might know and like already.”

He adds: “And then the real substance is in telling them our story – what led us to make each of the wines and why we think they are special.”

Ruel, who previously held a similar position for two decades with Napa Valley’s Trefethen Vineyards, also emphasises the importance of customer interaction, “working with them to find out what they like, and encouraging them to be open-minded but also confident in their own palates.”

Consumer tasting events

This approach not only applies to tasting room encounters and winery visits, but also when winery personnel travel to large consumer tasting events and wine dinners across the globe and when they are visiting retail shops.

“I always try to understand what consumers are interested in and focus on those topics,” says Eugenio Palumbo, director and head winemaker at Piemonte’s Vietti winery. “If they are interested in maps, I talk about the region. If they are more interested in tasting the wines, I offer wines that are approachable and drinkable.”

Mironov cautions: “Don’t make it overly technical unless they lead the conversation in that direction. The goal is to engage them, not alienate them with information and terminology they may not be familiar with.”

As Air Canada sommelier Véronique Rivest argued at a recent wine conference: “For too long, we have wrapped it [wine] in language that feels more intimidating than inviting.”

Too much information about philosophies, sustainability and certifications can also cause eyes and palates to glaze over.

Buyers and sellers

The first conversation that a wine producer has with a potential importer is often akin to a mating dance or a dating app – how should I look, and what do I say?

This dance often takes in place at rental booths at international trade shows such as Vinexpo Paris or ProWein where wine producers post signs saying “seeking representation,” hoping visiting importers will be tempted to stop, talk and taste.

If they do, importers will want to know price-points of each wine in their market and whether the quality of the wine justifies that price. Also important is the availability of goods over multiple years. But there is also a human element.

“There has to be someone at the winery who has reasonably good English and interpersonal skills so that they can travel to the US to represent the winery,” says Scott Ades, president of Dalla Terra, a major American importer of Italian wines, “but it definitely does not need to be perfect English, in fact it is better if it is not perfect. To develop a market perception of authenticity of the winery, someone from the winery needs to visit the market to tell their story.”

Once in the market, learning to communicate with wine shop owners and restaurant beverage managers may require a subtle shift and a broader palette of information.

“Scores, accolades, unique points of differentiation, pricing and a compelling quality-to-value story, along with supporting materials as needed, can all help,” Mironov advises. “Retailers are also interested in what will resonate with consumers on the sales floor. Information and materials that help them quickly communicate quality, style, occasion or value is often more useful than technical details. Food pairings can be especially important.”

Tackling preconceptions

Challenging or embracing pre-conceptions is an issue. “For wines from Virginia, no matter how good they are, we need to dispel old biases,” Ruel says. “Winegrowing has come a long way in this region and we’ve got the wines to prove it.”

For Donnafugata’s Rallo, “it is important for the trade to understand not only the individual wines, but also the architecture of the portfolio, the solidity of the project and its continuity over time. With the trade, the message becomes more structured.”

Whether at the winery or at the wine store, Vietti’s Palumbo says, “I focus more on technical tastings but never forget to talk about our philosophy, the production style, vineyards and our corporate identity.”

When it comes to restaurant sales, Mironov adds, “For on-premise buyers, the conversation is frequently more focused on how the wine fits within a programme. They want to know where it can sit on a list, what need it fills, how it complements the restaurant’s cuisine and overall concept. Also, is there a compelling story that staff can share tableside?”

Moore Brothers has wine stores in New Jersey, New York and Delaware with wines mainly from family producers, and Terry Moore says: “When considering someone new, we seek out producers who are respectful stewards of their land and whose wines convey a sense of that place—which not only requires incredible attention to detail in the vines throughout the growing season, but in the cellar, too.”

Media wars

Dealing with the media is more complicated because part of the time producers are desperately trying to get sceptical journalists to write about their wines, while at others times eager writers are trying to get detailed information from wine producers – and often very quickly. Having a public relations agency in the middle can at times facilitate the process and at other times needlessly complicate it.

To begin, many in the media find winery websites – which should be a producer’s best and least-expensive communications vehicle – annoying and incomplete.

“European winery websites are often lacking in basic information, and that is very frustrating,” says American wine journalist W. Blake Gray. “Every website should have at least the [basic] information easily available in an easy-to-find place, like an ‘About Us’ page. Who owns the winery – if it’s a family, name the family and each member who works there, who is the winemaker, and an email address for press inquiries.”

Having high-resolution, easily downloadable winery photos as well as product sheets on current vintages are invaluable.

A winery’s primary form of outgoing media communications is press releases and story pitches, often issued through agencies. “I find that writers are generally looking for what’s new, noteworthy or distinctive,” Mironov says. “They want to understand why a wine, producer, vineyard or region deserves attention and what makes the story relevant to their audience.”

And trying to convince media that every winegrower is a “visionary” on a mystical “journey” is worthless.

Offering samples to go along with pitches can be helpful, especially if a specific wine is being pitched. “It is somewhat insulting that someone would think that I would tout something I haven’t tasted,” says Michael Franz, editor of Wine Review Online.

Availability matters

Media also seeks primary sources – winery owners and winemakers – and not secondary sources. “I’m not interested in speaking to a brand manager or hospitality manager,” Gray says, except for basic information. “But I won’t quote them, and I won’t do a story based on an interview with them.”

What is especially frustrating for wine writers with a story assignment is trying to quickly arrange an interview, by phone or virtually, with a winemaker or winery owner and having their agency turning it into a production. This is especially irritating when an agency has even pitched the story with an offer to speak to the source. What should be a simple matching of schedules sometimes becomes an un-amusing “our team is talking with their team to get this done” and with requests for questions in advance “for what you have in mind” even when they pitched the idea.

What media loves is talking one-on-one with a person in charge, either when visiting a winery or while a winemaker is on tour. Here, intent can expedite matters.

“When we host media, we send out a form that asks them where their interests lie, what they are most interested in learning about,” says Katherine Jarvis, owner of Jarvis Communications.

Finally, Rallo believes that communicating with each audience, while different, is part of an integrated whole. “The task always remains the same – to create a connection between the wine and those who encounter it,” she says. “With consumers, this connection comes above all through emotion and experience; with the trade, through value, clarity and storytelling tools; with the press, through depth, transparency and the ability to tell the story of a project over time.”
